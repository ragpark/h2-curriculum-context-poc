How the arrangement works

One shared curriculum map, with teaching materials and pupil progress both linked to it

The curriculum map is a shared description of the subject: its topics, what depends on what, common misconceptions and teaching methods. Teacher's materials and pupils' answers are both tagged against the same map, so an AI tutor can see the pupil, the teacher and the curriculum together in one briefing. Teacher's materials

Tag the teacher's own materials against the map, with no teacher effort

Each material is split into teaching units. The tagging service tags every unit with concepts, the teaching method, the representation and any misconceptions it addresses. The dated tags then give the class's taught-so-far set and the teacher's preferred method. Tags below 0.5 confidence go to an optional review queue (dashed outline).

Pupil progress

Turn pupils' answers into a picture of their progress the tutor can use

Answers arrive from several sources. Questions from our own item bank are already tagged to topics on the map. Untagged markbook entries are interpreted by the tagging service. Everything goes into a log that is only ever added to. Each pupil's progress is then rebuilt, and the map spreads the evidence: success counts as weak evidence that earlier topics are secure, and wrong answers are traced to the misconception that caused them.

Evidence for the hypotheses

Evaluate

Two measures. First, tagging accuracy on three sets: the units the tagger was tuned against (optimistic), and two held-out sets. Held-out 2 is the honest figure, because it was written before the latest tagger change. Second, a hard tutor test: 12 pupil turns, three versions of the tutor's context, each reply scored blind on five 0–3 criteria against ground truth written by hand. The judge is an AI model, so treat its scores as indicative and confirm them with teacher review.

What is this for?

Imagine a supply teacher walking into your classroom. They know their maths. But they don't know your pupils. They don't know who keeps getting the same thing wrong, how you explained it last week, or what you haven't taught yet.

An AI tutor is in the same position. It is clever, but it walks in cold every time.

This prototype tests one idea for fixing that: give the tutor a set of class notes before it helps a pupil. To make those notes useful, everything is organised around one shared curriculum map. The map is a list of the topics in the subject, showing what comes before what and the mistakes pupils often make.

The big question: does a tutor give better help when its notes are organised around the curriculum map, compared with a pile of the pupil's answers and some worksheets? The Evaluate page tests exactly that.

Start here: a 5-minute tour

  1. Go to Overview and press “Run the whole demo”. This fills the prototype with the made-up class's worksheets and the pupils' answers. It takes a few seconds.
  2. Open “Curriculum map”. Each box is a topic. Lines show which topics you need first. Click a box to see common mistakes for that topic and how it can be taught.
  3. Open “Teacher's materials”. Click a worksheet to open it. Under each part you will see coloured labels. The computer added these by reading the worksheet: which topic it teaches, which method it uses, and which mistakes it tackles.
  4. Open “Pupil progress” and choose Amara. The map now shows what she can do (green) and where she is stuck (red). She keeps making the same sign mistake.
  5. Open “Tutor briefing”. Press “Assemble & ask tutor”. On the left is what a tutor gets from a pile of Amara's answers. On the right is the same information organised by the map. Compare the two.
  6. Open “Evaluate”. This shows how well the prototype did when tested. See “How to read the test results” below.

The second subject: English, Macbeth

Maths was the easy case: topics come in a clear order, like rungs on a ladder. English literature is not like that. Lady Macbeth, ambition and blood imagery are not steps; they are all connected to each other, and a pupil can meet them in any order. So we built a second map to test whether the idea still works. Use the Subject box at the top of the page to switch.

How the English map is different

  • Five groups instead of a ladder: the play's background (context), characters, themes, language and form, and writing skills.
  • “Related” links instead of “you need this first”. Click a topic and dotted lines show what it connects to. Only the writing skills build on each other: you need to pick good quotations before you can analyse them.
  • Key quotations are on the map, each linked to the characters and themes it shows.
  • The acts of the play are on the map. In English, “has this been taught?” is mostly about how far through the play the class has read. Lady Macbeth is taught in week 2, but her sleepwalking scene is in Act 5, which the class reaches in week 6.
  • Pupil evidence is marked written work, not right-or-wrong answers: the task, the topics it covered, the mark, and the mistake the teacher noticed, if any.

When you look at a class instead of a pupil, green means already taught and a dashed outline means coming later.

Coloured labels on worksheets and answers

concepta topic methoda way of teaching it repa picture or model used misca common mistake

The small number on a label shows how sure the computer is, from 0 (a wild guess) to 1 (certain). 0.9 is very sure; 0.5 is a coin toss. A label with a dashed outline means “not sure, a person should check this”.

Learning behaviour

pattern to support means we have seen the same unhelpful habit a few times, so the tutor should help with it. strength means a helpful habit we have seen a few times. These describe what we have seen recently, not what the pupil is like. They fade if they stop happening.

How to read the test results (Evaluate page)

The tutor test

We gave the same AI tutor the same pupil question three times, each time with different notes:

  1. No classroom context: the tutor knows nothing about the pupil or class.
  2. Raw classroom data: the tutor gets a pile of the pupil's recent answers and any worksheets that share words with the question.
  3. Organised by the map: the same information, sorted using the curriculum map, plus clear advice on what to focus on.

Another AI then marked each reply without knowing which notes the tutor had. It gave up to 3 marks for each of five things, so up to 15 in total:

DiagnosisDid it spot what this pupil actually needs?
Teacher's methodDid it explain things the way the class teacher does?
Respects what's been taughtDid it avoid jumping ahead to topics the class hasn't done yet?
Next stepWas the suggested next step right for this pupil?
True to pupil & classWas everything it said about the pupil and class actually true?

The learning-behaviour test adds a sixth: did it respond to how this pupil learns? For example, did it ask Isla to have a go before giving a hint?

Three things to keep in mind:
  • Small differences don't mean much. If you run the same test twice, scores move by about 1 mark. Only pay attention to differences bigger than that.
  • “Fresh” questions matter most. We improved the prototype using one set of test questions. Doing well on those is a bit like marking your own homework. So we also wrote a fresh set before making any changes, and only checked it afterwards. Trust the fresh results most.
  • The marker is an AI, not a teacher. It sometimes gets things wrong. Treat its marks as a useful first look, not a final verdict.

The labelling test

We labelled some worksheets by hand, then checked how often the computer agreed. “Concept tags correct: 93%” means that out of every 100 topic labels the computer added, about 93 were right. “Expected concepts found: 100%” means it didn't miss any of the topics we expected. Again, the Held-out set is the fairest test, because those worksheets were never used to improve the computer's labelling.

What the results say so far

Any classroom information makes the tutor's help much better: scores roughly double. Organising that information with the map helps a bit more, most clearly in getting the tutor to teach the way the class teacher does. A map that picks the wrong focus makes the help worse, so getting the map right matters.

What this can't tell you

  • Whether real pupils learn more. Everyone here is made up.
  • Whether teachers would agree with the marks. The marking was done by an AI.
  • Whether it works beyond one maths topic. Only solving equations has been mapped so far.
  • How it behaves with lots of messy, real data. Each made-up pupil has only a handful of tidy answers. Real schools have far more, from many different apps, and that is where the map may matter most.

Glossary

AI tutor
A computer program that chats with a pupil to help them learn, a bit like a teaching assistant.
Curriculum map
One shared list of the topics in a subject, showing which topics come first, the mistakes pupils often make and ways to teach each one. It's the same for every school.
Topic (concept)
One thing pupils learn, such as “solve equations with x on both sides”.
Builds on / comes before
A topic you need first. You need two-step equations before equations with x on both sides.
CASE
A published standard (from 1EdTech) for sharing lists of curriculum topics between computer systems. Each topic gets a permanent code and a web address, so different organisations can point at the same topic and say how their topics match up. The prototype can export its maps in this form.
Misconception
A common mistaken idea, such as forgetting to change the sign when moving a number across the equals sign.
Teaching method
A way of teaching a topic, such as the balance method or “change side, change sign”.
Representation
A picture or model used to explain, such as a bar model or algebra tiles.
Classroom context
The facts about one class and one pupil: what they answered, what the teacher taught and when, and the teacher's own worksheets. Different in every school.
Raw classroom data
Classroom context handed over as a pile, without sorting it.
Tag / tagging
A label that links a worksheet or an answer to a place on the map, like putting the right postcode on a letter.
Tagger
The part of the prototype that reads text and adds tags. There are two: a simple one that looks for key words, and a cleverer one that uses AI.
How sure (confidence)
A number from 0 to 1 showing how sure the computer is about a label. Higher means more sure.
Needs checking
A label the computer wasn't sure about, shown with a dashed outline so a person can check it.
Tutor briefing
The notes a tutor is given before it helps a pupil with one question.
Learning edge
The earliest topic the class has been taught that this pupil hasn't got yet. The best place to help next.
Secure / Developing / Gap
Can do it / getting there / struggling. Worked out from the pupil's answers.
Probably fine
We haven't seen the pupil do this directly, but they can do harder things that build on it.
Taught so far / Not yet taught
What this class has covered by this week, and what's coming later, taken from the dates on the teacher's materials.
Learning behaviour
How a pupil goes about learning: whether they plan, check their work, keep going, ask for help sensibly and know how sure to be. It's about how they learn, not what they know.
Pattern to support / Strength
An unhelpful or helpful habit we have seen a few times recently. Never a fixed label for the pupil.
Session only
Information that is used while the pupil is working and then thrown away, such as signs of frustration. It is never saved.
Teacher confirmed
A pattern a teacher has checked and agrees with. Only these would ever be shared beyond the tutor.
Scenario
A made-up pupil question used for testing, such as “I got it wrong again”.
Development / Fresh (held-out) questions
Development questions were used while improving the prototype. Fresh questions were written first and kept aside, so they give a fairer test.
AI marker
A second AI that marks the tutor's replies against notes written in advance, without knowing which version of the tutor wrote them.
Map version
The map gets updated from time to time, like a new edition of a textbook. Old answers still count after an update.
Links to other curricula
How a topic on the map matches up with, for example, the GCSE subject content.
Made up (synthetic)
Invented for testing. No real pupils, classes or teachers are involved.
H1, H2, H3
Programme shorthand. H2 is the curriculum map, H1 is pupil progress, H3 is teachers' materials.

Every topic has a permanent CASE identifier (a UUID derived from its readable ID, so it never changes) and a web address that resolves to its description. The readable ID, such as cc:maths/alg/lin-eq-both-sides, is kept as humanCodingScheme. The map, the MCP tools and the API accept any of the three forms.

/ims/case/v1p1/CFItems/{id}One topic. This is the address its identifier resolves to

What maps to CASE: topics, strands and acts become CFItems; prerequisites become precedes; related topics and close or broad crosswalks become isRelatedTo; exact crosswalks become exactMatchOf; retired topics become replacedBy. What stays in the map: misconceptions, teaching methods, representations and quotations. CASE has no native types for these, so they remain the publisher's teaching layer and point at the CASE addresses. Crosswalk targets use placeholder urn: addresses until those schemes are published in CASE.
